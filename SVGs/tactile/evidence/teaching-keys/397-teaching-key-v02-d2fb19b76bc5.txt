Batch 397 — essential anatomy teaching key

Three independent teaching models use enlarged skeletal outlines. This key is required to introduce their selected species, developmental stages, visible-crown conventions, grouped regions and omissions. Native thumbnails provide context, not specimen identification, microscopic counts, measured natural proportions or inherited approval. The drawings contain no visible words, numbers, Braille or logos.

Bat forelimb digit skeleton
The selected pattern is Carollia perspicillata, qualified to the juvenile RIGHT forelimb seen dorsally in Chen and colleagues' 2005 Figure 1B. Anterior is toward page top. Humerus is at left, elbow below it, and the radius returns up/right to the wrist. The short reduced ulna is incorporated into an introduced composite forearm outline with a proximal prominence; its distal part is fused to the radius. This composite glyph represents two related anatomical elements, not one bone or two equally long independent bones.

An aggregate wrist connects conceptually to five metacarpal rays, I–V. The free thumb ray I is short; II has a shorter distal series; III is longest, followed by IV and V. The selected juvenile phalange counts across I–V are 2, 1, 3, 2, 2. Each ray includes a separate metacarpal before its phalanges. The short thumb's external claw sheath is omitted, not counted as another phalanx. Wing membrane and complete animal body are omitted. The primary preparation includes cartilage; the drawing does not claim fully ossified adult bones or encode histological staining by tactile colour. The fused first-digit annotation in primary Figure 1C belongs to the HINDLIMB and is not applied to this forelimb.

Dog–horse dentition comparison
The upper example is an introduced domestic dog with fully permanent dentition; the lower example is an introduced adult male horse with permanent canines present and optional first premolars, or wolf teeth, absent. Both are nose-right lateral skeletal teaching examples with deliberately enlarged joint spaces and open-bite clearance. Their species, sex and stages are declared independent choices, not diagnoses of the tiny native skulls. Exact chronological ages, breeds, clinical occlusion and individual dental health are not asserted.

Each introduced skull distinguishes a posterior cranial vault from the narrower muzzle, with an orbital cue near the transition. The separate lower jaw has a substantial ascending posterior ramus, condylar joint region, coronoid projection and jaw angle continuous with its tooth-bearing body. These are sparse angular teaching outlines, not cortical tracings or clinical articulations. Tooth-root support baselines are schematic and joint/open-bite spaces are enlarged.

For the dog, each underlying upper hemiarch has three incisors, one canine, four premolars and two molars; each lower hemiarch has three incisors, one canine, four premolars and three molars. Across both sides the underlying permanent total is 42. One enlarged anterior incisor cue per visible arch represents the anterior group; it does not assert that only one incisor exists or that all three are visible. The near-side cheek series is drawn individually: upper P1–P4, M1–M2; lower P1–P4, M1–M3. The large upper P4 and lower M1 are the introduced carnassial/shearing cues. Canines are distinct from the smaller pointed premolars and reduced broad molars.

For the selected male horse without wolf teeth, each underlying upper and lower hemiarch has three incisors, one canine, three premolars and three molars, giving 40 across both sides. One anterior incisor cue and one canine are shown per visible arch. Each near-side broad cheek row contains six crowns in order P2, P3, P4, M1, M2, M3. The long diastema separates anterior/canine and cheek regions. Other horses can differ in canine and wolf-tooth presence; this is not a universal herbivore formula or an assertion that herbivores lack upper incisors or canines. Far-side teeth, individual incisor occlusions, roots, fine cusps, sutures and foramina are omitted. Mandibular condyle, jaw body, cranial outline and orbit remain introduced coherent regions rather than a clinical articulation map.

Four-member forelimb correspondence
From left to right: horse, humpback whale, human, selected Carollia bat. ALL FOUR members are required. Each compares the upper-arm element, forearm, grouped wrist and selected distal rays. The views have independent teaching scales and developmental qualifications; corresponding bones do not imply identical function, stage, natural proportions or a single universal five-digit adult pattern.

Horse: the adult ulna is reduced and partly fused with the radius. Main metacarpal III reaches one functional digit with proximal, middle and distal phalanges. Short metacarpals II and IV are splint remnants and terminate proximally; they are not two additional complete toes. Real sesamoids and individual carpal bones are omitted from this sparse correspondence model.

Humpback whale: Megaptera novaeangliae is represented by four complete rays II–V. Each has one metacarpal. The introduced phalange series is 2, 7, 6, 2, selected from the lower ends of the reported ranges 2, 7–8, 6–7, 2–3 in Cooper and colleagues' 2007 study. These values are a declared representative teaching selection, not a count measured together from one specimen, a universal species formula or an exact ossification stage. Many underlying study specimens were immature. Hyperphalangy is conspicuous in III and IV. A possible exceptional vestigial ray-I cartilage is not converted into a fifth functional ray. Individual carpals and soft flipper outline are omitted.

Human: humerus, separate radius on the thumb side and ulna on the little-finger side, an aggregate wrist, five metacarpals, and five digital series. The thumb has two phalanges and each other finger three. The wrist glyph groups the underlying eight carpals; it is not one carpal or a claim that all eight are individually visible. The ulna does not directly form a bony carpal articulation. This is an isolated skeletal forelimb, with explicit bone/body-part tags; no skin, hair, clothes, whole person or appearance is invented.

Bat: the same selected juvenile Carollia stage/count convention is separately introduced in this comparison. Five rays and 2/1/3/2/2 phalanges retain the reduced fused-ulna, short-thumb and elongated-fan relationships. Reusing this anatomical convention does not transfer source inspection or approval.

Carriers, media and physical limits
Tapered polygonal carriers represent selected skeletal elements, not literal cortical silhouettes. Inter-bone gaps and crown spacing are enlarged to keep relationships distinct. Grouped wrists are aggregate regions, not individual-bone counts. Black or grey does not encode natural tissue colour, bone/cartilage staining, species, sex or stage. Nonprinted human semantic references identify actual skeletal parts without adding skin boundaries or filled matter.

Nominal canvases are 680×560 mm, 1100×680 mm and 1380×800 mm, with one native unit per millimetre. At three pixels/mm, proof files are 2040×1680, 3300×2040 and 4140×2400 pixels. Millimetres and file/provider pixels are separate. These are selected nominal sizes, not proved minima, natural specimen scales or page/device-fit claims.

Swell uses black 1.4 mm outline carriers. ViewPlus uses the provisional grey principal-line role; actual calibrated height levels remain pending. Index dots are ordinary 1.2 mm line carriers, with original minimum 3.1 mm centre spacing and maximum 6.2 mm chord/native station spacing. They retain declared vertices, extrema, circle cardinals and contacts; they are not Braille or separate semantic filled points. The original 3.2 mm unrelated/outside-contact painted gap remains enforced. Source/form adequacy still requires fresh whole-image author and independent review; hardware/material calibration, physical fit, touch proofreading and learner recognition remain pending.
